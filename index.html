When the rain stops

How drought shows up in the paddocks of Australia's farming regions, and which regions feel it most. Thirty-three seasons of rainfall and wheat harvests, 1990-91 to 2022-23.

By Muhammad Aqila · October 2026 · FIT Data Visualisation 2
Before you start

When it doesn't rain, Australian farmers grow less. Most of us know that. What's harder to see is how much less, how often it happens, and whether every region suffers the same way. Below, twelve charts follow one question from the sky to the paddock: what does a dry season do to the harvest?

How to read the numbers. Many charts use a score from 0 to 100 that compares a region with its own history since 1991. 0 is the driest season on record for that region, 100 the wettest, and 50 is typical. That way a dry year in a desert region and a dry year on the coast both read as “low”.
Seasons run July to June, so “2002-03” means July 2002 to June 2003. The crop season is April to October, when winter wheat is growing.
1 · The pattern

Drought arrives in waves

Each row is a farming region and each column a season. Brown means a dry season for that region, teal a wet one. Look for dark vertical bands: 2002-03 and 2018-19 to 2019-20 were dry across most regions, while 2010-11 was wet almost everywhere. Switch to the crop season to see the months that matter most for wheat.

Blank rows are regions with no record for that season. Data: Australian Agricultural Drought Indicators (ABARES and CSIRO), built from Bureau of Meteorology rainfall.

2 · The map

Where the rain fell

Drag the slider and watch the dry patch move. Try 2018-19, when the brown spreads across the east, then 2010-11, when the wet swept back.

The pale regions have no rainfall record in this dataset. Regions are ABARES farm-survey regions, covering Australia's farming land rather than the whole continent.

3 · The link

Rain goes straight into the wheat

Each grey dot is one season in the chosen region: further right means a wetter crop season (April to October), higher up means a bigger harvest. The dots climb from bottom-left to top-right. The red path follows one drought and what came after, season by season: the harvest falls as the rain fails, then jumps back when it returns.

Rainfall: April–October rainfall in millimetres, averaged over the region, from SILO gridded data (Queensland Government, built from Bureau of Meteorology stations). Harvest: ABARES farm-survey estimate of wheat yield for the region (tonnes per hectare). Two independent sources.

4 · The cost

A dry season costs some regions far more than others

Each line is a grain-belt region. The left end is its usual wheat harvest and the right end is the harvest in the dry season you choose. In 2002-03 the average region harvested only about 46% of its usual crop. Red lines fell below 40%.

“Typical” is each region's middle harvest across 1990-91 to 2022-23. Labelled regions kept less than 45% of a typical harvest.

5 · Rain and harvest together

Where it was dry and the harvest failed

This map shows both at once. Dark blue means a dry season and a poor harvest, trouble on both counts. Pale grey is a wet season with a good harvest. Teal means the harvest held up despite dry weather; pink and purple mean a poor harvest even though rain was good, so something other than rain was at work. Try 2019-20, when the east and west were both in trouble, then 2010-11, when the east was wet with good harvests while the west's wheat belt was dry and struggling.

Each region's harvest is compared with its own record, and split into better, middling and worse thirds; rainfall is split the same way. Beige regions are outside the 12 grain-belt regions with harvest data.

6 · The biggest losers

Where a dry spell costs the most wheat

Each circle compares a region's harvest in its wettest third of seasons with its driest third. Bigger circles mean more wheat lost. NSW Central West loses the most, about 1.73 tonnes per hectare (64% of its wet-season harvest), followed by NSW Riverina (1.64 t/ha) and NSW North West Slopes and Plains (1.54 t/ha). The smallest loss is in WA Central and Southern Wheat Belt (0.41 t/ha).

A simple comparison of averages. Harvests also change with farming methods and prices over 30 years, so treat the size of each loss as a guide.

7 · Beyond the wheat

Drought rarely hits just one thing

Each line is a region, tracked across four measures: rainfall, how well pasture grew, how well wheat did, and how profitable farming was. The thick black line is the average of all regions. In 2002-03 that average sat low on every measure: 22 for rainfall, 25 for wheat and 15 for farm profit. In 2010-11 the same averages were 87, 77 and 77. Individual regions vary, and the thin lines show who got off lightly.

Rainfall is observed. Pasture, wheat and profit scores are modelled by the Australian Agricultural Drought Indicators (ABARES and CSIRO) from the weather. They show what the weather implies rather than what was measured in each paddock. 22 regions have all four.

8 · The whole picture

Dry years shift the whole harvest down

Each shape shows how harvests were spread across all 12 grain-belt regions and every season. In the driest third of seasons the typical harvest was only 65% of normal, against 100% in middling seasons and 119% in the wettest. The dashed line marks a normal harvest.

9 · The odds

A dry season puts the harvest at risk

In the driest third of seasons, 55 in every 100 regional harvests came in below 70% of normal. In the wettest third, only 2 in 100 did.

Each square is 1 in 100 region-seasons among the 12 grain-belt regions; 130 dry and 120 wet region-seasons.

11 · Beyond wheat

Every crop feels a dry year, except the irrigated ones

So far we have followed wheat. This chart asks whether other crops suffer too. Each row is a crop and each column a season, in five states. Brown means a poor yield compared with that crop's usual yield in the state, teal a good one. The dry seasons of 2002-03 and 2006-07 turn the winter crops brown in New South Wales and Victoria (and partly South Australia): wheat, barley, canola, oats and chickpeas fail together. The irrigated crops, cotton and rice, hardly change, because they are watered from dams and rivers rather than from the sky. Western Australia often runs the other way: 2010-11, a wet year in the east, was a poor one there.

Yield is production divided by area planted, from ABARES state crop data. “Typical” is each crop's middle yield in that state across the seasons shown. Seasons from 2022-23 are ABARES estimates, and the 2026-27 forecast is left out. Only crops with at least 25 seasons of data in a state are shown.

12 · The whole country

Zoom out: how dry was the whole continent?

The earlier maps showed farming regions. This one covers the whole country, in squares about 50 km across, and compares each square with its own usual rain during the crop season (April to October). Brown means less rain than usual and teal means more. In 2002-03 the typical square got only 27% of its usual rain, and 68% of squares got less than half. In 2010-11 the typical square got 182%, but the west missed out. 2018-19 was dry again (51% of squares below half), and by 2022-23 the typical square had 152% of its usual rain.

Rainfall: SILO gridded data (Queensland Government, built from Bureau of Meteorology stations). Each square is the average of about 100 small grid cells, compared with the 1991–2022 average for the same months. Very dry desert squares (usually under 25 mm in April to October) are left out. Thin lines mark the ABARES farming regions.

What it means

Weather sets the odds, and some regions carry more risk

Across the grain belt, the wettest seasons delivered above-normal harvests and the driest seasons often delivered less than two thirds of a normal crop. The cost is not shared evenly: the western wheat belt and the eastern grain regions did not always dry out in the same seasons, and some regions give up much more wheat than others when rain fails. These charts show a strong link between rain and harvest. They do not prove rain is the only cause, because prices, pests and farming methods matter too.

About the data

  • Rainfall and drought indicators: Hughes, N. (2024), Australian Agricultural Drought Indicators (AADI) regional indicator evaluation data, Mendeley Data, doi:10.17632/8yhcr28wbk.1. Method: Hughes et al. (2025), Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences 25, 3461. Produced by ABARES and CSIRO from Bureau of Meteorology gridded rainfall (bom.gov.au/climate/maps). Covers 1990-91 to 2022-23.
  • Wheat harvests: ABARES Australian Agricultural and Grazing Industries Survey (AAGIS) estimates by region, included in the dataset above. agriculture.gov.au/abares
  • Rainfall in millimetres (charts 3 and 12): SILO gridded monthly rainfall, Queensland Department of Environment, Science and Innovation, from Bureau of Meteorology station data, CC BY 4.0, longpaddock.qld.gov.au/silo. Averaged over each ABARES region. It agrees closely with the drought-indicator rainfall scores: the two rise and fall almost in step (correlation 0.94, where 1 would be a perfect match).
  • Region boundaries: ABARES AAGIS regions (ASGS 2016), simplified and reprojected for the web.
  • One region (NT Top End Darwin) has no data and is left blank. Wheat harvests exist for 12 grain-belt regions only.
  • Other crops (chart 11): ABARES, Australian crop report: September 2026, state crop data, doi:10.25814/5s62-8a44 (CC BY 4.0).
